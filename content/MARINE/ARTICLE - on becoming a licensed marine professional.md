#### Industry Mentors and Nepotism
The marine is not so unique that it lacks the shortcomings which affect many so many workplaces; nepotism. The marine industry has always been a sort of 'family career' where many generations of people will do the same work. I should preface this by saying that I don't have any actual resentment for anyone whose parents or family supports their career developments- I definitely do have a sense of jealousy towards those so fortunate, but I also think that anyone is capable of working hard and acknowledge that everyone within the workforce faces the same struggle to get by in a society that does not value the person as much as it values the worker. Everyone does what they must to get by, and as long as they aren't hurting anyone to get their piece I can respect the nobility of this struggle in anyone. I would also be lying if I said that I did not have any help while working towards my ticket; I was very fortunate to have the support of my senior officers and some captains while working on my higher education and I do not think that I would be in the same position I am in today without their help. There are a lot of people I work with who I am indebted to for their continued support and advice. I am often asking myself: how is anyone supposed to start out as a mariner if they don't already know someone who does this sort of work?

Nepotism within the marine industry does not present in the traditionally perceived sense where positions are handed out and tailored; due to the marine licensing system this also means that anyone who wants to become a mate or an engineer must go through the same process as everyone else. The nepotistic aspect of it all really just comes from the degree of mentorship that these sort of candidates are able to receive. A fine example of what I'm referring to would be young navigational cadets; in BC there is only an intake of about 20 students into the cadet program each year, so in order to pass through the program interview students must be well prepared for their interview with the school. Because of the experience needed to pass the interview, most cadets will first enter the workforce as deckhands in order to build experience and better understanding of the industry to their benefit. This also means that anyone who sees the 3/4 year cadet program as a 'fast track' towards their first ticket is forced to build real experience before pursuing this higher level of responsibility; and in my opinion this is for the better of the industry as a whole, because it means that more experienced people will move into highly responsible positions where they are responsible for the safety of their ship, crew, and the marine environment. In the case of the 'generational mariner', they tend to have a leg up because of their exposure to the industry through their family means that they are able to build perspective and understanding in a way which is not possible for most other people. Ultimately this sort of perspective and experience has no real benefit towards finding a position; but the faster that someone is able to finish their ticket, the sooner they will be in line for a position after a retirement.

#### Community Infighting
I am no stranger to being the recipient of churlish remarks within the local marine industry solely because of who I work for. Many times I have, perhaps foolishly in earnest, tried to enjoy my hard-earned position as a mariner within the community of mariners only to be laughed at and reduced to an employee of a certain employer, even when all of us have went through the exact same schooling to achieve our tickets. By its very nature, marine work is incredibly variable and no boat will ever be the same as another- but I have observed a sort of phenomenon where, in their shortsightedness and haste to flaunt their proficiency, mariners working on one type of ship will put down and continuously measure themselves against mariners on other ships. It seems like, rather than taking away a sense of unity in our shared struggles against bad weather, fatigue and pressure from our employers and government- everyone seems to be caught up in a dick-measuring contest that pits ferries versus tugs versus coastguard versus fishermen versus deepsea, and then all of us are just caught in this struggle for supremecy over one another instead of appreciating the fact that our jobs all suck in distinct ways. So many people are just completely focused on themselves and how they can best prove themselves at face value to others by asserting their dominance over a struggle that is always harder for them than it is for anyone else. Why is that? 
It is difficult for me to understand and even harder for me to accept. If in the process of getting my license I learned how to work on every kind of ship out there at one time, and everyone else also learned the same things; why does it matter if we ultimately settle on one sort of boat? I will admit that due to the nature of my work, my job is pretty comfortable- but why am I supposed to feel ashamed because I'm not scaling the end of barges with a 100lb cable over my shoulder, or because I'm not painting compartments while underway; and what is with the assumption that I also do not have the same foul days that everyone else has once in a while, where I am soaked through all my clothes in the rain closing hatches for heavy weather, or crawling into a rescue boat in below-freezing weather for regular drilling? I have an appreciation for the incredible variety of work that all boats are able to perform; I really do believe that in my entire life I could work on every kind of ship there is and still not get a complete understanding of everything about shipping, and this is also something that I love about this kind of work. I have no urge to defend my employer, but I can really appreciate the fact that because I work on passenger ferries that are except from pilot carriage regulations on standard passage, this also means that even as a junior officer I have the opportunity to gain shiphandling experience by docking myself, or coastal pilotage experience (that is sought out by pilots themselves) in large vessels; all of which are opportunities available to other ships of comparable size. Why is it fair that I am the target of homophobic insults, remarks about 'sex on the bridge', late sailings... etc when by all rights, I also have a wealth of unique experience at my fingertips?
